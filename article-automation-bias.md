# Automation Bias

Automation bias is inappropriate reliance on automated advice. A person may accept a wrong recommendation or miss a problem because the system did not flag it. Expertise and good intentions do not remove the risk.

Reliance depends on task, workload, interface, perceived reliability and available evidence. Avoid treating rubber-stamping as inevitable or assigning one detection rate to every workflow.

## Reduce the conditions that encourage it

Show source evidence and the concrete effect of an action. Make uncertainty and missing information visible. A confident explanation can persuade without making the result correct.

Reserve interruptions for decisions that warrant attention. Repeated low-value prompts can encourage habitual approval. Give reviewers enough time, manageable queues and authority to change the outcome.

For selected consequential judgments, ask the reviewer to form an initial view before revealing the recommendation. Such forcing functions can reduce reliance in some tasks, but they add effort and need evaluation.

## Measure behavior, not signatures

Seed plausible errors and observe whether reviewers catch them. Track misses, false alarms, review effort and outcomes. A signed approval does not establish that the reviewer understood or verified the action.

An [observational 2026 study of AI-code reviews](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721) reports rising approval alongside less commenting within repeat reviewers. That pattern is consistent with habituation, but an observational study cannot by itself establish its cause.

The [automation-bias references](codex.html#ref-F-7) provide the broader human-factors evidence. Apply it as a reason to test review design, rather than a reason to discard every human decision.
