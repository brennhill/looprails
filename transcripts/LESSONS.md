## 1. Error analysis comes before tests

- Jumping straight to writing tests is the failure mode. Ground tests in data analysis first, because with LLMs the surface area is larger and more stochastic than in normal software. — Lenny [00:10:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=627s)
- Open coding rule: write down **the first thing that is wrong, the most upstream error**, then stop and move to the next trace. Chasing every error in a trace kills throughput. — Lenny [00:21:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=1307s)
- Do not write "janky." Vague notes cannot be auto-categorized later, by an LLM or by you. — Lenny [00:42:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=2527s)
- 100 traces is a number chosen to unblock people, not a threshold. The real stop rule is **theoretical saturation** — stop when new traces stop producing new note types. Practitioners often saturate at 15–60. — Lenny [00:29:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=1796s), [00:30:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=1846s)
- **Benevolent dictator:** appoint one domain expert to do the coding, not a committee. Consensus processes make error analysis so expensive that teams stop doing it. — Lenny [00:25:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=1551s)
- An LLM cannot do the initial pass. Asked whether a trace has an error, it says the trace looks good, because it does not know the company offers no virtual tours. The hallucination is invisible without domain context. — Lenny [00:24:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=1444s)
- Axial coding is where the LLM earns its keep: dump the notes CSV, ask for categories. Then rename them. "Capability limitations" is too broad to act on. — Lenny [00:34:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=2091s)
- Give the categorizer a **"none of the above"** bucket. Anything landing there says the taxonomy is incomplete. — Lenny [00:43:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=2624s)
- The payoff is a pivot table. Counting is the most powerful and most undervalued technique in data science. The actual sheet (`frames/lenny-error-analysis-pivot.png`, [00:45:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=2705s)): 42 traces → conversational flow 17, human handoff/transfer 13, tour scheduling 8, formatting 2. A ranked work queue out of a pile of free text.
- **The same sheet proves the "don't write janky" rule.** Two rows of that pivot table are not categories at all — they are the categorizer's complaints, counted as if they were: *"The note 'Levi testing' is too vague to categorize"* and *"The category is unclear. The note 'VLA stated instructions' is too…"*. Two of 42 open codes were written too thin to survive the automated step, and they show up as noise in the very table you are trying to prioritize from. — Lenny [00:45:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=2705s)
- Once running, the loop costs ~30 minutes a week after 3–4 days of setup. — Lenny [01:30:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=5437s)

## 2. LLM-as-judge: the rules that make it trustworthy

- **Binary, one failure mode per judge.** A 1–7 scale is a way of not making a decision, and nobody can act on the difference between 3.2 and 3.7. Published "rate 1 to 7" judge prompts are treated as misinformation to be fought. A judge prompt built the right way (`frames/lenny-judge-prompt.png`): one failure mode, a definitions block, an enumerated list of what counts, TRUE/FALSE, "return exactly one token." — Lenny [00:52:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3155s), [00:53:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3191s)
- The reason judging works at all: **checking a granular checklist is a much easier task than generating the answer.** That is the answer to "isn't it just the same model marking its own homework?" — Copilot [00:31:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1895s)
- Micromanage the judge. A judge told "which is better" graded brace placement; a judge told "is there a test for null values, is there a test for an empty list" stayed on task and became usable. — Copilot [00:27:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1640s), [00:28:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1692s)
- **Never accept a bare agreement percentage.** For a failure that occurs 10% of the time, a judge that always says "pass" scores 90% agreement. Ask for the confusion matrix and drive both off-diagonal cells toward zero. A team reporting "agrees 75% of the time" with no matrix is a bad smell. Their own worked example, in two frames: the matrix (`frames/lenny-judge-vs-human-matrix.png`) is 73/18 over 1/8 on 100 traces, and the summary (`frames/lenny-judge-agreement-tpr-tnr.png`) reduces it to agreement 81.00%, TPR 88.89%, TNR 80.22%. The 81% is the number a team would quote; the 18 false positives are the reason it should not be. — Lenny [00:58:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3494s) → [01:00:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3642s)
- Judges are not just a pre-ship gate. Sample ~1,000 production traces a day through the same judge and you get a live quality measure. — Lenny [00:50:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3059s)
- Typical suite size: **4 to 7 judges.** Most failure modes are fixed by fixing the prompt, not by adding an eval. Write evals for the pesky ones — the behaviours you already specified and that still fail. — Lenny [01:04:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3884s)
- Judges age. Model output style shifted enough in a year that GAIA 2's comparison judges had to be rewritten. — Workshop [00:52:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=3174s)

## 3. Where evals sit in a product's life

- Build the prototype first. A vibe check answers "does this work at all" cheaply, and evals should not block shipping something wild. — Copilot [00:53:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=3239s)
- "**The purpose of building evals is not to have evals; it's to build products.**" Teams that go whole hog become eval companies that measure the wrong thing. — Copilot [00:54:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=3288s)
- If a found error has an obvious fix, fix it — don't write an eval for it. There is a cost/benefit line and finite time. — Lenny [00:46:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=2768s), [01:05:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3931s)
- Copilot's guardrail metrics — tens to low hundreds of them — accreted from past breakages. Each one is a post-mortem crystallized into a number that would have caught it a week earlier. — Copilot [00:56:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=3387s)
- The taxonomy the Copilot team used, in one slide (`frames/copilot-four-eval-types.png`, [00:08:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=532s)): **Algorithmic** — exact match, JSON schema conformance, length caps, parsable code fences. **Verifiable** — the code compiles, the SQL returns the expected rows, the tests pass. **LLM-as-Judge** — grounded in the reference document, contains expected facts, better/worse than a reference response. All three sit on a **Traditional A/B Testing** band. The slide asks its own open question under Verifiable: *"Pops up a lot in code gen. But elsewhere?"* — verifiable evals are the cheapest tier and the hardest to get outside code. The recap slide (`frames/copilot-four-eval-types-recap.png`, [00:43:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2581s)) maps the taxonomy onto what GitHub actually shipped: Harnesslib code completions on Algorithmic/Verifiable, chat completions on LLM-as-Judge, model/tokenizer/prompt changes on A/B testing.
- **You need two eval pipelines, not one.** One persona wants a canned run that emits a number for a ship decision; the other wants Lego blocks to prototype a product that does not exist yet. Forcing them into one pipeline serves neither. Notebooks for exploration, Python scripts for regression, Azure pipelines for automation. — Copilot [00:33:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1997s) → [00:34:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2050s), [00:37:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2257s)
- Evals are closer to PRDs than to unit tests: the judge prompt is the specification of correct behaviour, written from observed data rather than guessed up front. Expect it to change — you cannot know the failure modes in advance. — Lenny [01:01:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3663s), [01:02:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3738s)
- **Criteria drift** is the research finding behind that: in user studies, people's definitions of good and bad changed as they reviewed more outputs, and they invented failure modes only after seeing ~10 of them. This held for experienced LLM builders. ("Who validates the validators?", Shankar et al.) — Lenny [01:03:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3787s) → [01:04:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=3884s)

## 4. Building the offline dataset (Copilot's Harnesslib)

- The construction: take open-source Python/JS repos → run their tests → keep repos where everything passes → use coverage to map functions to tests → keep functions with a doc string, a covering test, and a line-count cap → scoop out the body → regenerate → rerun the test. Pass rate was 40–50% early on. (`frames/copilot-harnesslib-pipeline.png`) — Copilot [00:10:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=611s)
- **Contamination check:** repos had to postdate the model's training cutoff, and GitHub could get the training-set list from OpenAI directly. The slide reduces the whole exercise to four lessons (`frames/copilot-harnesslib-lessons.png`): test content not in training data, test content consistent with production traffic, test the entire system, keep the harness flexible. — Copilot [00:12:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=766s)
- That filter introduced its own bias: repos new enough to be uncontaminated are smaller than production code, so the eval set was systematically easier than real traffic. — Copilot [00:13:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=817s)
- Cache the **preparation**, not the inference: repo selection, test runs, function offsets stored in SQL and reused every run. — Copilot [00:44:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2674s)
- Python and JavaScript acceptance rates proxied surprisingly well for Haskell and Visual Basic. But without a historical eval run for a language you cannot diagnose a regression in it later — every diagnosis is relative to a past baseline. — Copilot [00:48:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2924s), [00:49:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2975s)

## 5. Metric traps found in production

- Optimizing completion acceptance rate alone produced lots of tiny completions and a worse experience. Metrics traded off against each other in ways that needed a data scientist to unpick, sometimes delaying a launch by a week. — Copilot [00:21:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1284s), [00:23:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1386s)
- The counterintuitive result: users **reluctantly accepted long completions and deleted the back half.** The team shipped deliberately shorter completions, against the founding intuition that Copilot should write as much correct code as possible in one shot. — Copilot [00:52:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=3136s)
- Keep the key metric list to about three (acceptance rate, characters retained, latency). Guardrails are diagnostic context for those three, not independent pass/fail gates. (`frames/copilot-production-metrics.png` — the three over a much longer guardrail list, all sliceable by language and locale.) — Copilot [00:18:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1080s), [00:19:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=1182s)
- **Confusion matrix over tool calls** shows which tools get mistaken for each other. Overlap means the tools don't partition the space; the same chart diagnoses wrong agent handoffs in a multi-agent system. (`frames/copilot-tool-call-confusion-matrix.png`) — Copilot [00:41:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2470s) → [00:42:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LwLxlEwrtRA&t=2523s)

## 6. The domain-expert lessons (Teresa Torres)

- Vibe checks said the coach was "pretty good." The first annotation pass produced "holy crap, my coach sucks." Same product, same person, one week apart — the difference was looking at the data in a structured way. — Teresa [00:11:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=713s)
- **The cheapest eval came from domain expertise, not engineering.** "Suggested a general question" reduced to a red-flag keyword list — a code assertion with no false positives that catches nearly everything she catches. An engineer would have reached for an LLM judge. — Teresa [00:18:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1114s) → [00:20:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1212s)
- **Context engineering by accident:** helper functions extract just the coach's suggested questions, so judges get small inputs. Cheaper, faster, more accurate — and the cached helper output means changing an eval doesn't rerun the expensive extraction. — Teresa [00:22:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1369s), [00:35:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2146s)
- **The eval was more correct than her own human labels.** Writing a specific eval forced her to codify a rubric she had never made explicit for herself; she then had to change how she labelled. She then scored the judges themselves (`frames/teresa-are-my-evals-any-good.png`, [00:29:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1799s)): TP/FP/FN with precision, recall and F1 per failure mode. A PM validating her own judges in numbers, one week in. — Teresa [00:26:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1581s) → [00:27:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1635s)
- One "failure mode" firing constantly was a bug in a helper function. Only the failure table exposed it. — Teresa [00:28:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1684s)
- Splitting one monster prompt into four LLM calls raised overall quality **and created a new failure mode** — each dimension became greedy and criticized questions belonging to another dimension. No change is uniformly better; every release is a judgment call over a table of small wins and small losses. The table she reads it off (`frames/teresa-fast-feedback-loop.png`) is one row per transcript, one column per eval, green/red — enough to re-run for a model, prompt, temperature or chunking change. — Teresa [01:04:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=3864s), [00:30:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1829s)
- **Annotate the whole trace, not one dimension at a time.** Per-dimension annotation surfaced many non-critical errors and no way to rank them. The customer receives the whole trace, so prioritization has to happen there. She lost significant time to this before switching. — Teresa [01:05:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=3915s) → [01:06:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=3965s)
- **81% → 3%.** An error spanning multiple LLM calls (the same excerpt reused across dimensions with contradictory verdicts) was fixed by passing each subsequent call the list of already-used excerpts. She had to build a detector for it first, because the repeated excerpts were not textually identical — then A/B test a workflow change rather than a prompt change. Three days end to end. — Teresa [00:43:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2594s) → [00:44:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2690s)
- **Build annotation tools; annotation you avoid is annotation you don't do.** Half a dozen custom tools, each spun up in about two minutes by Claude Code, with keyboard shortcuts and colour-coded traces. She stops mid-annotation to add affordances. — Teresa [00:40:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2402s), [01:06:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=4001s)
- Hamel's version of the same point: removing friction from looking at data is the highest-ROI activity available, and the custom viewer Nurture Boss built took a few hours. — Lenny [01:28:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=5288s)
- Start in the tool you already know (Airtable), not the eval platform. Limiting how much is new per step is what kept the project moving. — Teresa [00:10:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=616s)
- Two tiers of AI-assisted coding, deliberately separated: **eval and production code is pair-programmed and read line by line; throwaway annotation UIs are vibe-coded and tested only at the boundaries.** Rationale: fear of a product she cannot maintain. — Teresa [00:31:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=1891s), [00:40:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2444s), [00:39:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2348s)
- Notebook markdown is maintained for the human and the model at once — well-sectioned, documented notebooks work better as LLM context. — Teresa [00:37:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N-qAOv_PNPc&t=2249s)

## 7. The coding-agent exception

- Coding agents are the atypical case, and generalizing from them is a mistake: the developer *is* the domain expert, uses the product all day, and can read the output directly. That collapses activities other products must keep separate. — Lenny [01:13:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=4412s)
- "We just use vibes" from a coding-agent team rests on foundation-model evals someone else ran, plus informal internal error analysis. — Lenny [01:11:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=4317s)
- Dogfooding does not transfer: doctors are not out there soliciting bad medical advice from an assistant all day. Most domains have no dogfooding population. — Lenny [01:14:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=4462s)
- Foundation-model benchmark scores do not correlate with product-specific failure modes like botched human handoffs. — Lenny [01:21:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BsWxPI9UM4c&t=4896s)

## 8. Agent evaluation: what breaks at the agent layer

- **Capability and reliability are different axes.** Across 14 frontier models, accuracy on GAIA and τ-bench rose steeply while composite reliability crawled. The chart (`frames/workshop-reliability-lags-capability.png`) plots Google, Anthropic and OpenAI models from May 2024 to Dec 2025: the reliability trend line moves from about **0.70 to about 0.80 in 19 months**, while capability over the same window is not close to that flat. Slide caption: more recent models are far more capable and only slightly more reliable. The dense follow-up (`frames/workshop-three-panel-reliability.png`) breaks it into accuracy against release date, reliability against release date, and reliability against accuracy, with fitted slopes per lab. Offered as the explanation for why crushed benchmarks have produced no measurable deployment. (Rabanser et al., "Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability", 2026.) — Workshop [00:27:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1650s) → [00:28:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1724s)
- The four reliability dimensions, measurable on **any** benchmark: consistency (outcome, trajectory, cost stability), robustness (injected faults, semantically-preserving prompt rewrites), predictability/calibration, failure severity. Consistency is defined operationally (`frames/workshop-consistency-overview.png`): run the same task K times, then ask whether the outcome, the trajectory and the resource cost hold across runs. — Workshop [00:26:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1577s) → [00:34:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=2063s)
- 70% accuracy is ambiguous: it can mean 70% of tasks always succeed, or every task succeeds 70% of the time. For a customer-service agent those are entirely different products. — Workshop [00:26:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1577s)
- Calibration has improved (labs were burned by overconfident chatbots) while **discrimination has got worse** — agents cluster their confidence near the middle instead of separating successes from failures. — Workshop [00:31:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1868s)
- Agents confuse a messy process with a wrong answer: tool-call failures en route make a model doubt a correct result. — Workshop [00:32:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1964s)
- **Reliability thresholds should gate automation, not augmentation.** A coding agent's errors are cheap because a reviewer is in the loop; the same error rate in an autonomous customer-service agent is not. A 10% wrong-order rate is dead on arrival. — Workshop [00:24:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=1482s), [00:33:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=2012s)
- **The harness can matter more than the model.** Six frontier models sat within a couple of points of each other on SWE-bench Pro while harness choice moved results by 22%. Terminal-bench shows the same model scoring differently across harnesses. If you are comparing models you may be comparing harnesses. — Kaggle [00:18:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=1119s), Workshop [01:09:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=4195s)
- **Agent identity is a black hole.** Evals are tagged with a model name, but the agent is model + sub-agents + MCP servers + memory config. Without those fields nothing is reproducible. Nor do the environments compose (`frames/workshop-benchmarks-incompatible.png`): Tau-Bench, WebArena and TerminalBench expose incompatible protocols, so cross-environment evaluation needs ad-hoc engineering per benchmark. — Workshop [00:14:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=853s)
- Two agents with an identical pass can differ in steps, cost, wall-clock and error count. Session-level reporting, not just a score. The slide makes it concrete (`frames/workshop-two-agents-same-pass.png`): two agents both marked PASS, one at 3 steps / $0.04 / 12 seconds / 0 errors, the other at 18 steps / $0.41 / 4 minutes / 2 crashes. Caption: the score just says "pass", nothing else. — Workshop [00:11:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=707s)
- Also unreported by default: seeds, prompt perturbations, pass@k, cost. — Workshop [00:15:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=901s)

## 9. Reading other people's eval numbers

- A published SWE-bench score omitted 40 of 237 problems, disclosed in fine print. The chart crime shown alongside it (`frames/workshop-misleading-bar-chart.png`) is a stacked bar: a with-thinking segment stacked on 52.8 makes it read as taller than a competitor's 69.1 — the tweet on screen asks "which is larger, 52.8 or 69.1?". A second tweet in the same frame flags a 72.5 vs 74.5 comparison published with no error bars. — Workshop [00:04:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=268s) → [00:06:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=367s)
- Across **171 model release documents**, first-party transparency has fallen since 2022; under 15% now mention labour and environmental effects. Practitioner interviews attribute it to disbanded documentation teams and legal/political pressure to report capability over risk. Third-party evaluation quality and volume rose over the same period. The decline is a colour-graded grid of per-quarter reporting scores (`frames/workshop-reporting-transparency-over-time.png`); the claim does not survive as prose. Reading one public leaderboard row by row, with per-row notes on shots and agent used, is worked through at `frames/workshop-hle-leaderboard.png`. — Workshop [00:06:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=417s) → [00:08:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=515s)
- A competing lab reran a published benchmark using its own API's compaction and reported much better numbers. The configuration, not the capability, moved the score. — Kaggle [00:03:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=206s)
- Leaderboards go stale by construction: authors publish and move to the next benchmark. ~10 new benchmarks appear per day. PvP formats (Elo, models playing each other) never saturate because there is always a winner and a loser. — Kaggle [00:02:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=158s), [00:06:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=400s)
- Statistical significance is a budget line: 400,000 poker hands for one game benchmark, reduced via Bradley-Terry pairing. — Kaggle [00:14:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=881s)
- Benchmarks are built by roughly 30,000 AI researchers for a world of billions. Anything nobody benchmarks cannot be hill-climbed — hence the jagged capability profile. The example that makes it concrete: a wastewater-treatment engineer in Turkey built a safety benchmark from 20 years of incident experience that exists nowhere else. — Kaggle [00:04:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=255s) → [00:05:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ubwb6NzegyA&t=302s)

## 10. Environment and verifier design

- **Prefer hard verifiers.** GAIA 2 replaced rubric/LLM judging with event-by-event comparison of the agent's action diagram against an annotated expected diagram — cheaper and more reproducible. LLM soft verifiers are reserved for free-text content like an email body. The combined pipeline (`frames/workshop-hybrid-hard-soft-checking.png`) runs the hard verifier and the LLM judge into one verified score, described as action-level rewards with high precision and reused as an RL training environment. — Workshop [00:50:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=3028s) → [00:51:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=3078s)
- GAIA 2 results by capability: search and execution are largely solved; **time-based tasks sit near 0% for every model, including frontier ones**; adaptability and ambiguity are weak. (`frames/workshop-key-findings-table.png` — flagged on air as already outdated.) — Workshop [00:52:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=3127s)
- Three deliberate noise injections: tool failures, **renamed API signatures** (catches agents overfitting to specific tool names), and unrelated environment events. — Workshop [00:48:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=2930s)
- Simulate rather than use the live world: reproducible, safe for destructive tasks ("delete all my emails"), cheap, and time can be fast-forwarded so a three-week scenario does not take three weeks. — Workshop [00:44:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=2642s), [00:48:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=2882s)
- **Reward hacking is the default failure of a lazy grader.** Agents fix the unit test instead of the bug. Grade everything you asked for — ask for three things and grade two, and the third gets skipped. Fingerprint tests; deny the agent access to grader and solution. — Workshop [01:01:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=3663s), [01:06:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=4001s), [01:07:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=4049s)
- Weight your unit tests. A pile of trivial passing tests can hide the few that represent the actual job. — Workshop [01:00:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=3615s)
- Beyond success rate: steps, tokens, latency, cost, and pass@k. Then read traces, and run automated failure analysis over trajectories to find the slice where success rate collapses. The two slides (`frames/workshop-agent-metrics.png`, `frames/workshop-failure-analysis.png`) carry the worked example: a model averaging 80% on a harness drops to 50% on one task category, which is the case for slicing rather than reporting one number. — Workshop [01:08:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=4099s) → [01:11:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UxMZfbWI3LY&t=4297s)

## 11. Experiment design, from the phone-agent paper

- **Change exactly one variable.** Same tasks, same random seeds, same scoring; only the interface differs (screenshots+taps vs terminal text+shell). (`frames/phone-controlled-comparison.png`) — Phone [00:04:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=280s)
- Grade on final world state with a rule-based verifier, no model in the loop — both sides are judged on whether they changed the world correctly, not on how they narrated it. — Phone [00:04:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=286s)
- **Measure the ceiling, not just the score.** Hand-built oracle solutions (must pass the verifier, must reach the answer by real exploration, three independent professionals must sign off) showed 89% of Android World tasks are terminal-solvable at an average of 3.7 steps — against live agents taking ~15. The headroom, not the headline, is the finding. (`frames/phone-canyon-of-headroom.png` for the step gap, `frames/phone-triple-signoff-rule.png` for the sign-off rule that makes an oracle count.) — Phone [00:08:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=480s) → [00:08:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=531s)
- **Scaffolding pays off inversely to base-model strength.** Escaping-safe tools gave one weaker model +12–14 points and lower cost; strong models moved by about a point. Gate tool investment on model strength rather than adding tools everywhere. — Phone [00:11:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=676s)
- **A benchmark can only contain what its interface can express.** Mobile benchmarks were built around the screen, so an entire class of tasks was invisible to every score. Cross-app tasks cap at 11% for screen agents and scale does not move it — one screenshot per step is a structural bottleneck, not an intelligence one. (`frames/phone-cross-app-wall.png` — the screen agent at a flat 0% on cross-app beside the terminal agent collapsing both apps into one workspace, labelled *structural*.) — Phone [00:12:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=773s), [00:15:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=918s)
- The paper's self-critique is the model to copy: the terminal side got a hand-written system prompt, a four-phase loop and custom tools while the screen baselines ran as-is — "the two contestants didn't get the same coaching" — and the retained benchmarks all grade final device state, which favours the terminal. A plausible task suite is not a representative one. (`frames/phone-benchmark-selection.png` — benchmarks kept because they check final state, AndroidLab excluded because it checks interface structure.) — Phone [00:18:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=1116s) → [00:20:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=1212s)
- Cost and privacy stated plainly: ~$8,000 for the paper's experiments, and a terminal agent is a privileged process that reads private storage past the permission prompts and ships it to a cloud provider each step. — Phone [00:21:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=1260s), [00:21:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3sZRAStjDs&t=1307s)
